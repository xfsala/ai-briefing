How to read this site

What is this?

A short, regular briefing on AI for people who need to understand what it means, not only what was launched. It is written from high-calibre newsletters and research journals, with every point read through a behavioral-science lens focused on the Saudi region.

How is a briefing laid out?

Opening question: something to think about before reading, about what the news means for people.

Reflection: a short line on how these developments could help people grow and societies progress, without ignoring the risks.

In one line: the single shift worth remembering if you read nothing else.

Top stories: four or five stories, each with what happened, why it matters, and a suggested action.

Saudi and region lens: how the news connects to Saudi digital infrastructure, the economy or policy. When there is no real link, the page says so.

Project ideas: two ideas side by side, each with a first step: a general idea that could become a research or consulting project, and something to create for children aged 15 and under.

Watchlist: people, companies and organisations worth tracking, tagged by sector.

Tool to try: only when something is genuinely useful for research or data work.

Verify before quoting: bold claims to check before repeating them to anyone.

How is a research radar laid out?

Papers of the week: five to eight studies chosen for their relevance to behaviour, trust, health, policy, work and culture. Each shows the real title, the journal, the type of study, a short summary and why it matters.

Preprint or journal article? A preprint has not yet been checked by other scientists (peer review), so treat its findings as early. Journal articles have passed peer review.

Patterns this week: what several studies point to together.

How should I judge a headline?

Newsletters are free because your attention is the product, so headlines are written to be clicked. Ask four questions:

1. Who says so? A company announcing its own product is not independent evidence.

2. Measured on what? Benchmarks are often narrow tests chosen by the company.

3. Available to whom? Many launches are US-only, limited previews or waitlists.

4. What would change my decision? If the answer is nothing, it is a Watch, not a Try.

How should I judge a study?

1. What kind of study is it? A randomised experiment shows cause and effect; a survey shows what people say or do at one point in time.

2. Who took part? Results from one country or group may not hold in Saudi Arabia.

3. Has it been peer reviewed? Preprints can change before they are published.

4. How big is the effect? A finding can be real but too small to matter in practice.
